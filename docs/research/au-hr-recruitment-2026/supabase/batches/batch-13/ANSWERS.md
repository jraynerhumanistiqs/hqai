## RA8
<!-- Right ad channel(s) & cost as an SME -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: FALSE
- status: review

### answer_text
Match the channel to the role before you spend anything. For high-volume or entry-level roles, start with free or low-cost job boards. For specialist or niche roles, targeted channels such as industry boards or professional networking sites may earn their cost. The trap is defaulting to the most expensive channel for every role, then posting once and giving up. Treat your first ad as a test. If the right people are not applying, change the wording or the channel mix and try again. On hard-to-fill roles where you are competing for candidates, budget for paid visibility upgrades. Estimate how much of your own time the hire will take, so you know when an agency fee is worth it. Treat published cost comparisons with caution, because most come from job boards and agencies. Whichever channel you use, the same rules about what your ad says still apply.

### source_refs
SEEK Employer - Hiring advice: Hiring trends to watch (https://talent.seek.com.au/hiring-advice/article/hiring-trends-to-watch)
Appetency Recruitment - Best Hiring Channels: Where to Advertise Jobs in Australia (https://www.appetencyrecruitment.com.au/blog/best-recruitment-channels-for-small-businesses-where-to-advertise-jobs-in-australia)
ScaleSuite - Hiring Costs by Channel: LinkedIn vs Agencies vs Referrals (Australia) (https://www.scalesuite.com.au/resources/hiring-cost-by-channel-australia)

### escalation_guidance
If you are unsure whether your ad wording could exclude people on a protected attribute, such as age, sex or family responsibilities, get the content checked before it goes live, because discrimination risk applies on every channel. If an agency is involved, read its fee and replacement terms before you sign, since those are contractual commitments.

## RA15
<!-- Salary negotiation & counter-offers -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Decide your negotiation room before the ad goes live, so the advertised figure is not your absolute ceiling. Know your top number before a candidate asks, not while they are waiting on the phone. Negotiating after an offer is now normal, so expect it rather than reading it as a bad sign. If their current employer counter-offers, you have three choices: match, hold firm, or walk away. In shortage roles, plan for this early so you do not lose a signed candidate at the last minute. The trap is the one-off exception. Before you go up for one person, check what your existing staff doing similar work are paid, and write down why this offer is different. Ad hoc, undocumented pay decisions are the same habit that creates equal-remuneration exposure later.

### source_refs
Robert Half - 2026 Australia Salary Guide / negotiation insights - https://www.roberthalf.com/au/en/insights/career-development/how-negotiate-higher-salary-offer-email-sample-included

### escalation_guidance
Get advice before going ahead if the offer would put a new starter above existing staff doing similar work, especially where the gap falls along gender lines. Undocumented one-off pay decisions are what equal-remuneration claims are built on.

## RA4
<!-- "Unicorn candidate" / wishlist inflation -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Before you post the ad, split your list into two columns: true must-haves and nice-to-haves. Must-haves are the safety, legal and technical minimums the job cannot be done without. Everything else is a nice-to-have. Then ask whether the list is really one role or secretly two, and check it against the pay you can actually offer. This is the small-business trap: you can only afford one hire, so you try to fix every gap with one person. Asking for ten years' experience on a junior wage, or a mix of tools and tickets that rarely come together, leaves the role empty while you pass over good people who tick most boxes. Inflated requirements can also tip into indirect discrimination if they are not genuine requirements of the job and they screen out a protected group, such as unnecessary physical, language or experience thresholds.

### source_refs
SEEK Employer - Unicorns: Who Makes the Ideal Candidates? (https://sg.employer.seek.com/hiring-advice/article/unicorn-candidates)
GWG Blog - How to spot (and secure) a unicorn candidate (https://gwg.com.au/blog/how-to-spot-and-secure-a-unicorn-candidate/)

### escalation_guidance
If a requirement could screen out people by age, sex, disability, race, language or another protected attribute and you cannot show the job genuinely needs it, get advice before you advertise or shortlist. A requirement that is not genuinely needed for the job can create indirect discrimination exposure.

## RA16
<!-- Reading a bifurcated market -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: FALSE
- status: review

### answer_text
Before you set a budget or timeline, check whether the role is on the Jobs and Skills Australia Occupation Shortage List, and think about how your region compares. Hiring has got easier overall, with more applications per ad, but health, trades, early childhood and school teaching have stayed tight. That is the trap. Commentary about corporate or tech hiring tells you little about finding an electrician or a nurse. For a shortage role, allow more time to fill and review your offer, including pay and what else makes the job worth taking. Think about widening your search by region, skills-based hiring or visa sponsorship. For a role with plenty of applicants, expect volume and put your effort into screening rather than advertising. Either way, do not let urgency push you into skipping proper screening or right-to-work checks.

### source_refs
Jobs and Skills Australia - 2025 Occupation Shortage List
Australian Bureau of Statistics - Labour Force, Australia (latest release)

### escalation_guidance
Get specialist migration or employment advice before you make an offer if you are thinking about visa sponsorship or are unsure how to check someone's right to work. Mistakes there are hard to undo once the person has started.

## RA18
<!-- Time-to-fill blowout & vacancy cost -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Treat a hard-to-fill role as a project with a deadline, not something that will turn up. Decide early whether it is a shortage or niche role, especially in a regional area, because those take much longer than the national median of roughly a month. If it is, put in more effort and budget from day one: turn ads around fast and book interviews quickly. At the same time, set an interim cover plan, such as overtime, a temp, or redistributing work. Then set a date when you will widen channels, lift spend or bring in an agency if the role is still open. The trap is treating months of cover as business as usual. Sustained understaffing raises fatigue and safety risk and can push good staff to resign, sometimes with a claim attached. Check in regularly with whoever is covering.

### source_refs
SEEK Employer - Hiring trends to watch (https://talent.seek.com.au/hiring-advice/article/hiring-trends-to-watch)
SmartCompany - Wrong hires costing Australian SMEs, SEEK research, Feb 2026 (https://www.smartcompany.com.au/people-human-resources/wrong-hires-costing-australian-smes-7-3-billion-year-seek/)

### escalation_guidance
If staff covering the gap report fatigue, have a safety incident or injury, or say they are leaving because of the workload, get advice before you act. Sustained understaffing creates WHS exposure and can lead to resignation or constructive dismissal claims.
