## X6
<!-- Time poverty - admin crowding out the business -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Sort HR tasks by risk before you sort them by urgency. Routine items like a standard leave request can be batched into a set time each week and handled from a template. Anything involving a termination, a complaint or an audit letter gets your full attention straight away. The trap is treating every HR question as equally urgent, so low-risk admin eats the same anxious energy as the jobs that can actually hurt you. The other trap is leaving compliance admin until a deadline or complaint forces it, because that is exactly when mistakes happen under time pressure. When a question comes up for the second time, build a standing process or template for it so you only solve it once. If a question is genuinely new, that is the one worth getting one-off advice on.

### source_refs
CCIWA - Big stress in small business: new survey reveals rising pressure (2025 Small Business Conditions Survey)

### escalation_guidance
If the item on your desk is a termination, a complaint or an audit letter, it is not admin to batch. Get advice before you act, because a rushed mistake there creates legal exposure that saved time will not undo.

## X3
<!-- Escalation boundary - People track -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Get advice before you act, not after a claim arrives. Routine paperwork, everyday performance conversations and standard leave questions are generally fine to handle yourself. Bring in a human professional if the matter could end in dismissal with disputed facts, or involves discrimination, a bullying or harassment complaint, a redundancy or restructure, or an enterprise agreement. The trap is that a performance chat and a disciplinary process heading towards dismissal look the same in the moment. Watch timing. Acting on a termination soon after they raise a complaint, a health issue, pregnancy, family responsibilities or a leave request is itself adverse action risk, whatever your real reason. Redundancy means the job no longer exists, not that you would rather they left, and the Small Business Fair Dismissal Code does not cover it. If a complaint names a senior person or you, use an external investigator.

### source_refs
Fair Work Commission - Dismissal rules for small business owners
Fair Work Commission - General protections and harmful (adverse) action
Safe Work Australia - Guide for Preventing and Responding to Workplace Bullying
Australian Human Rights Commission - Assistance and making a complaint

### escalation_guidance
Any possible dismissal, redundancy, restructure, or discrimination, bullying or harassment complaint, and any adverse decision close in time to a complaint, leave, illness, injury or pregnancy - get advice from an employment lawyer or qualified HR practitioner before you act. Once a letter is sent or an informal chat has happened, your options narrow and claims are cheap to lodge.

## X7
<!-- Confidence barrier - "accidental HR" psychology -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Before you act on anything that affects someone's pay, job or safety, run it past a second, qualified source. Not knowing what you don't know is normal when HR landed on you. The trap is that the regulator gives no discount for 'we didn't know', and silence from staff is not proof you are compliant. Sort your questions by stakes. Everyday questions about awards, leave and the Fair Work Act are often answered free by the Fair Work Ombudsman, so check there before paying for advice. Anything touching dismissal, discipline, pay you may have got wrong, complaints, pregnancy, illness or injury needs a qualified answer before you move, not a mate's opinion or a forum post. Keep a short checklist and notes of past decisions so you rely less on memory and gut feel.

### source_refs
Fair Work Ombudsman - fairwork.gov.au (free guidance on pay, awards and entitlements)
Fair Work Act 2009 (Cth)

### escalation_guidance
Any decision about dismissal, discipline, possible underpayment, a complaint, pregnancy, illness or injury - get a qualified answer before you act, because a wrong move there cannot be undone by apologising.

## X1
<!-- HR tech gaps & "spreadsheet-and-hope" workflows -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Pick one place where each employee's records live and make it your official record this week. That covers their contract and any changes to it, timesheets, leave balances, and who agreed to what, when. It does not need to be expensive software, but it cannot be one person's inbox or memory. The trap is that spreadsheets seem fine until a Fair Work audit, an underpayment claim or a dismissal dispute exposes every gap at once. Poor records weaken your position in a dispute. Fair Work Inspectors can issue infringement notices for record-keeping failures alone, separate from any underpayment. The Small Business Fair Dismissal Code checklist only protects you if it was actually completed and kept. Reconcile leave balances regularly and keep one current copy of each policy and contract. When award rates change, check your pay against the Fair Work Pay Calculator.

### source_refs
Fair Work Ombudsman - Record-keeping and infringement notice powers (fairwork.gov.au)
Fair Work Commission - Small Business Fair Dismissal Code
Hall & Wilcox - What every Australian business needs to know about employee records (hallandwilcox.com.au/news/what-every-australian-business-needs-to-know-about-employee-records/)
MDC Legal - Employers need to keep employment records (mdclegal.com.au/news/employers-need-to-keep-employment-records/)

### escalation_guidance
If tidying your records shows pay or leave may not have matched the award, or the gap comes up during a Fair Work contact, underpayment claim or dismissal dispute, get advice before you correct anything. Mishandling the fix can turn a records problem into an underpayment or unfair dismissal exposure.

## X2
<!-- Record-keeping & documentary-evidence gaps -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Start keeping records now, before anyone asks for them. The Fair Work Act requires you to make and keep employee records for seven years, covering pay, hours, leave, superannuation and more. Beyond that, write a short dated note after every performance conversation, disciplinary meeting or verbal warning. A verbal warning is not informal just because it was spoken. If you dismiss someone and you are a small business, complete the Small Business Fair Dismissal Code checklist at the time, not after a claim arrives, because a checklist filled in later carries much less weight. For any redundancy, keep a record of the consultation and the redeployment options you considered. Keep old contract versions and your award-rate calculations too. The trap is that nobody tells you until an inspector or tribunal asks, and by then missing records usually count against you.

### source_refs
Fair Work Act 2009 s535
Fair Work Ombudsman - Small Business Fair Dismissal Code

### escalation_guidance
Get advice before you act if records are missing or incomplete and you are facing a dismissal, redundancy, unfair dismissal claim, underpayment query or Fair Work Ombudsman request. Do not backfill or recreate documents after the fact, because missing records can decide the matter against you.
