## RA3
<!-- Vague need -> accurate job description -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Start with outcomes, not tasks. Write down the three to five things this person must deliver for the business, then list the duties that produce them. Split skills into must-have and nice-to-have, and decide who they report to and at what level. Do not just reuse your old ad or an online template. The trap is describing what the last person did rather than what the role needs now. Add a simple success measure for each outcome, because you cannot later manage performance against duties that were never accurately written down. Before you advertise, check the duties against the right award classification, because an inaccurate description is where classification errors and underpayment start. Finally, decide whether this document also becomes the contract schedule or stays a separate recruitment document.

### source_refs
Michael Page AU - 5 common hiring pain points every hiring manager in Australia can relate to (https://www.michaelpage.com.au/recruitment-expertise/employer-insights/5-common-hiring-pain-points-every-hiring-manager-australia-can)
Development at Work Australia - 41% of Employers Experienced Pain Points in their Recruitment Efforts this Quarter (https://www.developmentatwork.com.au/2026/02/20/recruitment-pain-points/)

### escalation_guidance
If you are unsure which award or classification the duties fall under, or the role is being rewritten for an existing employee, get advice before you advertise or change anything. A wrong classification creates underpayment exposure that backpay alone may not fix.

## RA2
<!-- Choosing engagement type without misclassification risk -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Decide the engagement type from how the work will actually be done, not from what is cheapest or simplest. If you will set their hours, supervise them, supply the tools, and they will work only for you, the law is likely to treat them as an employee whatever the contract says and whether or not they have an ABN. The trap is asking for an ABN as a condition of the job, or 'trialling' someone as a contractor. That can be sham contracting, which is unlawful under the Fair Work Act. Getting it wrong can mean back-paying leave and super, civil penalties for each breach, and tax and super guarantee exposure with the ATO. Casual is not a free option either, because casual conversion rights still apply. Write down why you chose the engagement type, and look at it again if the arrangement starts to change.

### source_refs
Fair Work Ombudsman - Sham contracting (https://www.fairwork.gov.au/find-help-for/independent-contractors/sham-contracting)
Fair Work Ombudsman - Sham contracting in the spotlight, media release March 2026 (https://www.fairwork.gov.au/newsroom/media-releases/2026-media-releases/march-2026/20260313-sham-contracting-media-release)
ATO - Sham contracting in the spotlight (https://www.ato.gov.au/media-centre/sham-contracting-in-the-spotlight)

### escalation_guidance
If you are unsure whether someone is a contractor or an employee, or a contractor has drifted into regular hours for you alone, get advice before you engage or renew them. Misclassification can mean back-pay, per-breach civil penalties, and ATO super guarantee liability.

## RS14
<!-- Right-to-work / visa verification (VEVO) gaps -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Check every new starter's work rights in VEVO before their first shift, and check every one of them, not just the people you assume might be on a visa. VEVO is a Department of Home Affairs system that confirms visa status, work conditions and expiry, with the person's consent. The Migration Act makes it an offence to employ someone without work rights, and penalties apply per worker. The trap is treating it as a one-off. Time-limited visas expire and carry conditions such as work-hour limits, so diary a re-check before each expiry date. Do not take their word for it, and do not assume a recruitment agency or labour hire firm has done it for you. Asking only candidates who 'look' like migrants is itself discriminatory, so use the same wording and the same check for everyone.

### source_refs
Department of Home Affairs - Work-related contraventions (immi.homeaffairs.gov.au, Employing and sponsoring someone / Employer responsibilities)
Department of Home Affairs - Visa Entitlement Verification Online (VEVO)
Migration Act 1958 (Cth)
Migration Amendment (Strengthening Employer Compliance) Act 2024 (Cth)

### escalation_guidance
Get migration or employment-law advice before you act if a check shows someone has no work rights, has breached a visa condition, or has a visa about to expire. Acting wrongly can mean ending employment unlawfully, and continuing can mean a per-worker offence. Also get advice if you have been checking only some candidates, because that is a discrimination risk.

## RS7
<!-- Unlawful / discriminatory interview questions -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Ask about the job, not the person. If the role needs weekend shifts, travel or physical work, say so and ask whether they can do it. Do not ask about age, pregnancy or family plans, kids, marital status, health, religion or where they are from. The Fair Work Act stops you refusing to employ someone because of attributes like these, and state and territory anti-discrimination laws add more, with the list varying by state. The trap is the friendly chat: "just making conversation" questions are where people slip. If you do not hire them, a question about their kids can look like the reason. Questions tied to a genuine requirement of the role are fine. Candidates can decline to answer. They can complain to the Fair Work Ombudsman, Fair Work Commission or Australian Human Rights Commission. Check AI-drafted or generic question banks before you use them.

### source_refs
Fair Work Act 2009 (Cth) s351
HRM Online - Illegal interview questions: be careful what you ask

### escalation_guidance
If a candidate complains, or you have already asked a question about a protected attribute and then decided not to hire them, get advice before you respond. A refusal to employ linked to that attribute is adverse action and discrimination exposure.

## RS8
<!-- Unstructured, gut-feel interviewing -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Before you interview, write down the four or five things the job actually needs, then write the same core questions for each one and ask every candidate those questions. Write down their examples during the interview, not from memory afterwards, and score each answer against the criteria before you compare people. Gut feel is the trap. 'Loved their energy' or 'didn't feel right' are poor predictors of how someone will do the job, and they are where bias slips in most easily. They are also the hardest hiring decisions to defend. If a candidate you knocked back claims discrimination or adverse action, asking different people different questions with no written record leaves you nothing to show the decision was about the job. You can still have a friendly chat. Just do it on top of the structure, not instead of it.

### source_refs
Fair Work Ombudsman - Protection from discrimination at work / general protections

### escalation_guidance
If a candidate who missed out raises discrimination or adverse action, or you suspect a protected attribute influenced the decision, get legal advice before you respond, because an unstructured interview with no notes is very hard to defend.
