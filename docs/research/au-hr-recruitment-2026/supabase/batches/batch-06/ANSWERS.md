## RA6
<!-- Job ad that attracts the right applicants -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Write the ad so the wrong people rule themselves out. Keep it short and use a simple structure: a two-line role summary, what a normal day actually looks like, the must-haves (only the genuine ones), what you offer, and the salary. Leaving the salary out tends to cut both the number and the quality of applicants. Add a few screening questions that test the must-haves so you are not reading every CV. Drop jargon and generic template phrases. A plain, honest picture of a small business helps the right people see themselves in the role. The trap is skipping the final read-through. Check the ad describes the real job and that a job actually exists, because misleading ads can breach the Australian Consumer Law. Check the wording for anything discriminatory, and check the advertised pay meets the award for the work.

### source_refs
ACCC - Misleading job and business opportunity ads
Competition and Consumer Act 2010 (Cth) Sch 2 (Australian Consumer Law) s31

### escalation_guidance
If the ad sets requirements tied to age, sex, family status, disability or similar, or advertises pay you have not checked against the award, get advice before posting. Discriminatory wording or an under-award advertised rate creates exposure that cannot be fixed after applicants respond.

## RA7
<!-- Avoiding discriminatory language/criteria in ads -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Before you publish, check each requirement: does the job genuinely need it, or is it standing in for age, sex, race, disability, pregnancy, family or carer responsibilities, or another protected attribute? Describe what the person must be able to do, not who they are. "Young and energetic" and "recent graduate only" point at age. Instead of "native English speaker", state the level of English the work actually needs. The trap is the disguised version. An experience threshold the role does not need, a physical requirement the job does not involve, or "cultural fit" used to screen people out can be indirect discrimination even when nothing says it outright. Do not ask about kids, pregnancy or health in the ad or early screening. Federal and state or territory laws apply side by side, and complaints can go to the Australian Human Rights Commission and on to court.

### source_refs
Attorney-General's Department - Australia's anti-discrimination law
Fair Work Commission - Discrimination
Age Discrimination Act 2004 (Cth)
Sex Discrimination Act 1984 (Cth)
Disability Discrimination Act 1992 (Cth)
Racial Discrimination Act 1975 (Cth)

### escalation_guidance
If a rejected applicant complains, mentions a protected attribute, or lodges a complaint with the Australian Human Rights Commission or a state body, get legal advice before you respond. If you believe a requirement linked to age, sex, disability or similar is genuinely needed for the role, also get advice before you advertise, because a wrong call here is a discrimination claim.

## RA11
<!-- Setting a defensible pay band without enterprise data -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Start by finding the right award and classification for the work the person will actually do, then check the minimum in the Fair Work Pay Calculator. That is your floor, not your answer. Next, look at the market: free recruiter salary guides and current job ads for similar roles in your area. Use more than one source, because the guides are produced by recruitment agencies and can run high, and one competitor ad is an anecdote, not a benchmark. Allow for location, since regional and city rates can differ. Then set a band by experience level, leaving room to negotiate without paying a new starter more than people already doing the same job. The trap is classification. Get it wrong and no advertised salary protects you, because paying below the award or the National Minimum Wage is unlawful whatever was agreed.

### source_refs
Fair Work Ombudsman - Pay and Conditions Tool (Pay Calculator)
Fair Work Ombudsman - Find your award

### escalation_guidance
If you are unsure which award or classification covers the role, or you suspect current staff in similar roles are paid below the award, get advice before you set the band. A wrong classification creates underpayment exposure that grows with every pay run.

## RA12
<!-- Whether/how to advertise salary (transparency) -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
You do not have to put the salary in the ad. There is no general legal requirement in Australia to disclose it. But leaving it out now has a cost: ads with a visible salary draw noticeably more applications, and many candidates read "competitive salary" as a red flag. If you publish, pick a fixed figure or a range. Keep any range tight enough to show you know what the role is worth, and match it to your approved budget, or expect friction at offer stage. Decide whether you are quoting base only or the full package with super and any bonus, and say which. Two traps: the ad must not misrepresent the pay, and any figure you print must not sit below the award or minimum for that role. Check the award before you post. Staff may notice the number, so know where your current pay sits.

### escalation_guidance
If you are unsure which award covers the role or whether your advertised figure meets its minimum, check with the Fair Work Ombudsman or an adviser before posting. Advertising or offering below the minimum creates underpayment exposure.

## RA13
<!-- Avoiding illegal/underpaid job-ad rates (FWO) -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Before you post any job ad, check which award covers the role and the classification the person will actually work at. Then confirm the current minimum rate with the Fair Work Pay Calculator. Advertising a rate below the award, enterprise agreement or national minimum wage is a specific offence in its own right. Fair Work Inspectors actively search job sites and issue fines, and enforcement is increasing. The trap is the rate you have always paid. Award rates go up each year after the annual wage review, so an ad that was compliant last year can be illegal now. If you advertise a flat rate, it must genuinely cover every penalty and loading the person would otherwise get, and you should be able to show how. Re-check your ads and templates whenever rates change.

### source_refs
Fair Work Ombudsman - Job ads (https://www.fairwork.gov.au/starting-employment/job-ads)
Fair Work Ombudsman media release - Fair Work Ombudsman crackdown on dodgy job ads, June 2024
Fair Work Ombudsman media release - Employers warned over dodgy job ads after doubling of fines issued, October 2025

### escalation_guidance
If you are unsure which award or classification covers the role, or you find you have been advertising or paying below the minimum, get advice before you hire or adjust pay. A non-compliant ad can mean a fine, and the same mistake in actual pay is underpayment exposure.
