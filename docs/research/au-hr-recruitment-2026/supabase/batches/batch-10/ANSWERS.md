## PF4
<!-- Sham contracting -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
If someone works for you as a contractor, check that the arrangement is real in practice, not just on paper. Sham contracting is misrepresenting what is really employment as an independent contracting arrangement, usually in a way that avoids award, NES and super obligations. It is a separate contravention from simple misclassification, and it carries its own penalties. The trap is thinking you are safe because you never meant to do it. Recklessness counts, and whether your belief was reasonable is judged objectively. A signed contractor agreement is not a complete defence, and neither is 'everyone in my industry does it this way'. Review long-standing contractor relationships, especially ones that have changed over time. Penalties are significant, and there can be flow-on super and tax exposure. The regulator has named construction and road transport as priority sectors.

### source_refs
Fair Work Act 2009 s357
Fair Work Ombudsman - Nearly $200,000 in penalties for sham contracting involving workers with disability (media release, 30 July 2024)
Fair Work Ombudsman - Sham contracting in the spotlight (media release, 13 March 2026)
Department of Employment and Workplace Relations - Compliance and enforcement: Civil penalties and sham contracting factsheet

### escalation_guidance
If a worker challenges their contractor status, or you are deciding whether to reclassify or end any contractor arrangement, get legal advice before you act. A wrong move can turn a classification problem into penalties, back-pay and super exposure.

## PF7
<!-- Casual conversion - Employee Choice Pathway -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
When a casual tells you in writing they want to go permanent, note the date and reply in writing within the deadline the Fair Work Act sets. Missing that deadline is the trap. You no longer have to review casuals and offer conversion yourself. Under the Employee Choice Pathway the employee starts it. They become eligible after six months, or twelve months if you have fewer than 15 employees. You can say no, but only on valid grounds. Examples are the person still genuinely meeting the casual definition, or conversion needing substantial changes to how you run the business. 'I'd rather keep her casual' is not a ground. Write your reasons down and think about talking with them before you refuse. Check the current response period on the Fair Work Ombudsman site. Getting it wrong can mean civil penalties and a Fair Work Commission dispute.

### source_refs
Fair Work Act 2009 Part 2-2 Division 4A (casual employment - employee choice)
Fair Work Act 2009 s15A
Fair Work Ombudsman - Casual employees
Master Builders ACT - Employee Choice Pathway - Casual to Permanent Conversion
DLA Piper - New casual conversion changes are now in force

### escalation_guidance
If you plan to refuse, the deadline is close, or the employee disputes your reasons or raises it with the Fair Work Commission, get advice before you reply. A late or poorly grounded refusal carries civil penalty exposure.

## PF2
<!-- Fixed-term contract limits (2-yr cap, one renewal, FTCIS) -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Before you offer or renew a fixed-term contract, check two things: will the total time with this person pass two years, and is this already a renewal? Generally a fixed-term contract cannot run past two years including renewals, cannot be renewed more than once, and cannot roll over automatically. Exceptions exist, such as genuine seasonal work, specific-task funding and high-income employees, but the list is narrow and technical, so do not assume you fit. The trap is back-to-back contracts: earlier contracts with the same person count towards the cap. A term that breaks the rules has no effect, and the job may become ongoing by law. The Fair Work Act requires you to give the Fixed Term Contract Information Statement with every new fixed-term contract. Using fixed terms to sidestep redundancy or NES obligations is a target, so if the work is ongoing, offer an ongoing job.

### source_refs
Fair Work Ombudsman - Fixed term contract employees
Fair Work Ombudsman - New Fixed Term Contract Information Statement
Fair Work Act 2009 ss333E-333N

### escalation_guidance
If the person is already past two years or a second renewal, you are relying on an exception, or you plan to let a contract end instead of renewing it, get advice before you act. The job may already be ongoing by law, so ending it could be a dismissal or redundancy.

## PF16
<!-- Parental leave & the govt PPL interaction -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Treat this as two separate things. The NES gives eligible staff up to 12 months' unpaid parental leave, and they can ask for a further 12 months. The government Paid Parental Leave scheme is run by Services Australia, not by you. You do not fund it, and you do not have to pay them while they are away unless your own policy offers paid leave on top. Super on government Paid Parental Leave is paid by the ATO, not through your payroll. Topping up super during unpaid leave is your call. The trap is the return. They have a right to come back to the same or an equivalent role, and refusing leave or failing to honour that right breaches the NES. Check the notice and evidence steps, and check Services Australia for the current scheme length, which is expanding.

### source_refs
Fair Work Act 2009 ss67-85
Services Australia - Paid Parental Leave scheme changes
ATO - Paid Parental Leave Superannuation Contribution
Department of Social Services - Super on Paid Parental Leave and expansion of the scheme

### escalation_guidance
Any refusal of parental leave, any change to their role on return, or any discipline or dismissal around pregnancy or parental leave needs legal advice before you act. Getting it wrong risks an NES contravention and a general protections or discrimination claim.

## PF17
<!-- Long service leave - state-by-state -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Work out which state or territory the person mainly works in, then use that state's long service leave law, not the Fair Work Act. Long service leave is not part of the NES. Each state and territory has its own law, and they differ on how many years someone needs before they qualify, how much leave they get, when a part payout is owed if they leave early, and what pay the leave is calculated on. Where the work is mainly done generally decides which law applies, not where your head office is. A remote worker interstate may be under different rules from the rest of your team. The trap is applying one state's rules to everyone, or missing a pro-rata payout when someone resigns or is made redundant before the full qualifying period. Check the relevant state law before you pay anything out.

### source_refs
Long Service Leave Act 1955 (NSW)
Long Service Leave Act 2018 (Vic)
Industrial Relations Act 2016 (Qld) Chapter 2 Part 3

### escalation_guidance
Any termination, resignation or redundancy of someone with several years' service, or any worker based in a different state from you, needs a check of that state's law and payout rules before final pay. Getting the pro-rata entitlement or base rate wrong is an underpayment with back-pay and state penalty exposure.
