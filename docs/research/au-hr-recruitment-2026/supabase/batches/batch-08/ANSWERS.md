## RS16
<!-- Offer letters/contracts missing essential terms -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Before the person starts, put the offer in writing and make sure it covers the role, whether they are full-time, part-time or casual, start date, ordinary hours, location, pay, superannuation, probation terms and notice provisions. Give them the Fair Work Information Statement too, and the Casual Employment Information Statement if they are casual. A quick email confirming a start date and salary is the trap. It feels done, but it leaves gaps that surface later as a dispute, usually over notice, probation or entitlements. If you never set out probation or notice terms, you may find you cannot rely on them when it matters. Not handing over the Information Statement can itself be a breach. 'We'll sort the formal contract later' rarely happens, so send the full terms with the offer. Use a standard template, and check pay against your award.

### source_refs
Fair Work Ombudsman - Fair Work Information Statement
HQ.ai AU HR research report - G1. Offer letters and contracts missing essential terms

### escalation_guidance
If a dispute has already started over notice, probation or entitlements for someone hired on an informal or verbal offer, get advice before you act. Relying on terms that were never written down can weaken your position or lead to a claim.

## RS1
<!-- Resume overload with no triage system -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Before you open a single application, write down a short list of must-have criteria and a separate list of nice-to-haves, all tied to the actual job. Then screen against that list only. Anyone clearly missing a must-have goes in the no pile, and you read the rest properly. Set your cut-line by how many people you can realistically interview, not by how tired you are on Sunday night. The trap is reading first and working out what you want later. That is how screening turns into skimming for keywords, or rejecting someone over a typo or formatting that has nothing to do with the role. Keep a one-line note of why each person was cut. It takes seconds. If a rejected candidate later alleges discrimination, an unrecorded decision is hard to defend.

### source_refs
HQ.ai AU HR research report - A1. Resume overload with no triage system

### escalation_guidance
If a rejected candidate complains, alleges discrimination, or asks why they were cut, get advice before you reply. How you screened and what you recorded will matter.

## RS3
<!-- No consistent CV scoring rubric -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Before you open applications, write down the criteria for the role, split into essential and desirable, and decide how much each one counts. Then score every application against that same list and keep the scores. Most small businesses screen on gut feel. The problem is less your instinct than that you cannot explain it later. Because the same person often screens, interviews and decides, nobody else checks the reasoning. The trap is writing the criteria after you have already picked someone, or changing them halfway through. If a rejected applicant raises a discrimination or adverse-action complaint, a written rubric applied the same way to everyone is your main defence. Without one, a complaint becomes a real legal exposure. AI screening tools and new privacy rules are also adding pressure to explain screening decisions in writing.

### source_refs
HQ.ai AU HR research report - A3. No consistent scoring rubric for CV screening

### escalation_guidance
If a rejected applicant complains, raises discrimination, or questions why they were not shortlisted, get advice before you respond or recreate any records. Writing criteria after the fact to justify a decision can turn a complaint into a real claim.

## X8
<!-- Where SMEs currently go for answers -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Before acting on an HR answer, decide what kind of question it is. If it is general or factual, like what an entitlement is or how to calculate it, the Fair Work Ombudsman website and small business helpline are free and authoritative, and its Employer Advisory Service gives free tailored written advice. If it turns on your specific facts or the stakes are high, general resources cannot answer it for you. The trap is a confident source that is not qualified. Your bookkeeper can run payroll, but that is not employment-law expertise. A mate or a Facebook group may be describing a different state, award or situation. Some commercial ads have been found to mislead small businesses into thinking they were a government service, so check who you are talking to. Bad advice usually surfaces at claim or audit time, when it costs far more to fix.

### source_refs

### escalation_guidance
If the matter involves a dismissal, general protections, bullying or sexual harassment, or any fact-specific situation where a claim or back-pay is possible, do not rely on Google, forums or your bookkeeper - get tailored advice before you act. Eligible small businesses may get free legal help through the Fair Work Commission's Workplace Advice Service.

## X5
<!-- Cost barrier - prohibitive/opaque legal fees -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Before you call anyone, put the key facts, dates and documents on one page, then get two or three quotes or fixed-fee offers before you commit. Quotes are standard and cost nothing. Match the help to the question. Routine compliance questions often suit an HR consultant, which is usually much cheaper than a lawyer. A one-off question suits a single fixed-fee consult, while an ongoing need may suit a retainer. Ask whether a lawyer can review your document rather than handle the whole matter. If the amount in dispute is smaller than the cost of fighting it, settling may be the better business call. The trap is waiting until a problem feels big enough to justify the bill, because by then the cheap options have closed. The opposite trap is going it alone once a dismissal, investigation or underpayment is live.

### source_refs
HQ.ai AU HR research report - 6.5 Cost Barrier - Legal/Consultant Fees Are Prohibitive and Opaque

### escalation_guidance
Once a matter involves a dismissal, investigation, discrimination or harassment complaint, or possible underpayment, get professional advice before you act. Saving on fees at that point routinely costs far more in penalties, back-pay or settlement.
