## PF19
<!-- Paid family & domestic violence leave -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
If someone tells you they need time off to deal with family and domestic violence, give it to them and keep it quiet. All employees, including casuals, can take 10 days of paid family and domestic violence leave in a 12-month period. The full 10 days are available upfront, not built up over time, and unused days do not roll over to the next year. The trap is the payslip. Do not write 'family and domestic violence leave' on it. Show the time as ordinary hours or another neutral description, because naming it is a separate breach of the confidentiality rules. You can ask for some evidence, but keep it limited and do not demand more than you need. In a small team you may already know their situation, so be careful about who else learns anything. Check the Fair Work Ombudsman fact sheet for what evidence is reasonable.

### source_refs
Fair Work Act 2009 ss106A-106E
Fair Work Ombudsman - Paid family and domestic violence leave
Fair Work Ombudsman - Family and domestic violence leave fact sheet
Department of Employment and Workplace Relations - Paid family and domestic violence leave resources now available to support small business employers

### escalation_guidance
If you are thinking of refusing the leave, the employee's safety or injury is involved, or their details may already have been disclosed, get advice before you act. Denying the leave or breaching payslip confidentiality each carry civil penalty exposure.

## PF12
<!-- Annualised salary arrangements & reconciliation -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
If you pay an award-covered employee a flat salary, keep a record of the hours they actually work and check the salary against what the award would have paid. A generous salary does not cover everything on its own. Many awards, including the Clerks award, have an annualised wage clause. Where it applies, you need to write down which award entitlements the salary is meant to cover, set an outer limit on the hours or penalties it absorbs, and reconcile at least once a year. If the salary fell short, pay the difference. Skipping the reconciliation is the trap: failing to reconcile or keep records is a breach in itself, separate from any shortfall. A contractual set-off clause is only an option where your award allows it, and the wording has to be precise. Check your award's own clause before relying on either.

### source_refs
Corrs Chambers Westgarth - Annualised salaries: what are the options? https://www.corrs.com.au/insights/annualised-salaries-what-are-the-options
Parke Lawyers - Annualised Salaries and Modern Awards: Common Mistakes Employers Make https://pl.com.au/information-centre/annualised-salaries-modern-awards
Holding Redlich - Key changes to modern award annualised salaries https://www.holdingredlich.com/key-changes-to-modern-award-annualised-salaries

### escalation_guidance
If a reconciliation shows a shortfall, hours were never recorded, or you are relying on a set-off clause you are not sure your award supports, get advice before you act - this is underpayment exposure and the back-pay obligation does not go away.

## PF23
<!-- Timesheet accuracy feeding award interpretation -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Pay from the hours people actually worked, not the roster. Keep a record of real start and finish times for every shift, including the unrostered late finish. That one entry can decide whether overtime or a penalty rate was owed at all. Trusting your staff is fine, but trust is not a record. The Fair Work Act sets a record-keeping obligation, and correct award pay, overtime, penalties and super all depend on the hours data underneath. When the roster and actual hours don't match, check and correct before the pay run, and talk to the person about any disagreement. The trap is rounding. Rounding that always lands in your favour is a specific red flag for the Fair Work Ombudsman. Estimates and gaps also make any later dispute about hours much harder for you.

### source_refs
Fair Work Ombudsman - Record-keeping and pay slips fact sheet
Fair Work Act 2009 s535

### escalation_guidance
If you find past pays were calculated from rostered rather than actual hours, or an employee disputes the hours you paid, get advice before you fix it. That is now a possible underpayment, and how you handle it matters.

## PF28
<!-- Single Touch Payroll (STP) compliance -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
If you pay employees, report each pay run to the ATO through STP-enabled payroll software. A spreadsheet or manual payroll will not do it. STP applies to essentially all employers regardless of size, and every pay run is its own reporting event, so it is not something you tidy up once a year. Each report sends the ATO salary and wages, PAYG withholding and super information, and it replaces the old end-of-year payment summaries. The trap is thinking reporting is paying. STP is an information obligation only. You still have to pay the PAYG withholding and the super separately. If you find a mistake in a report you have already lodged, correct it promptly rather than leaving it. Missing or wrong reports can bring penalties and a higher chance of an audit and back-payments.

### source_refs
Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth)

### escalation_guidance
If you have missed STP reports, cannot correct a lodged report, or have reported PAYG or super you have not actually paid, speak to your accountant or registered tax or BAS agent before you fix it. Unpaid super or withholding can mean back-payments and penalties.

## PM16
<!-- Defensible informal/low-level investigation -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
When someone complains, act promptly and write the complaint down. Then talk to the complainant, the person accused and any witnesses separately, and take notes. Give the accused the specific allegation and a genuine chance to respond before you decide anything. Weigh the evidence, reach a finding on what is more likely than not, tell both people the outcome, and follow up to check it has settled. The trap is acting on the first account you hear. Doing nothing is just as risky: bullying and conflict are a recognised psychosocial hazard under WHS laws, which vary by state. If you are close to either person, or are one of them, be honest about that and consider whether someone more independent should run it. A sloppy process can also undermine any later discipline or dismissal that relies on it.

### source_refs
Safe Work Australia - Psychosocial hazards

### escalation_guidance
Get advice before you act if the complaint involves harassment, sexual harassment, discrimination or a protected attribute, if you are one of the parties, or if the findings could lead to discipline or dismissal. A mishandled process can become a WHS or general protections claim in its own right.
