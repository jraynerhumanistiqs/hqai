## PM5
<!-- Designing & running a defensible PIP -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Before you start a PIP, be honest about whether you have already decided to let them go. If you have, the PIP is a trap for you, not them. A PIP is not a legal requirement, but if you use one and then dismiss, the Fair Work Commission looks hard at the process, not just the outcome. Make sure the problem is capacity, not misconduct. Set specific, measurable targets they can realistically hit, and a timeframe that suits the role. There is no fixed legal period. Name the support you will give, schedule regular check-ins and write down what was said at each one. Tell them plainly what happens if targets are not met. Decide the outcome at the review date, not before. A dismissal after a PIP that was vague, rushed or unsupported can be found unfair even if performance really was poor.

### source_refs
Fair Work Act 2009 s387

### escalation_guidance
Get advice before you start any PIP that could end in dismissal, and before deciding the outcome, especially if the person has raised a complaint, taken leave, is ill or injured, or is pregnant. A flawed PIP process can make a dismissal unfair even when performance was genuinely poor.

## PM3
<!-- Which policies are mandatory vs advisable -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Sort your policies by legal weight before buying any template pack. No law requires an employee handbook, but several obligations effectively force written policies: WHS systems for hazard reporting, consultation and incidents; an anti-harassment policy, your key evidence of 'reasonable steps' under the Sex Discrimination Act positive duty, which has no small-business exemption; a privacy policy once privacy law applies to you; and a grievance process. Then document leave, rostering and pay processes so they match the NES and your award. WHS law varies by state, and Victoria has its own. The trap is reading 'no handbook needed' as 'no policies needed'. Other traps: a years-old template never updated for the right to disconnect, psychosocial hazards or the positive duty, and no record that staff received and acknowledged it. Check policies against the award and contracts in force, and review them when the law changes.

### source_refs
Australian Human Rights Commission - What are your obligations?
Sex Discrimination Act 1984 (Cth) s47C
Fair Work Act 2009 (Cth) Part 2-2 (National Employment Standards)
Privacy Act 1988 (Cth)
Safe Work Australia - Model Work Health and Safety Act

### escalation_guidance
If you have received a harassment, discrimination or bullying complaint, or an inquiry from the AHRC or a WHS regulator, get advice before relying on or rewriting policies, because missing or outdated policies can be used as evidence you did not take reasonable steps.

## PM8
<!-- Unfair dismissal & Small Business Fair Dismissal Code -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Before you dismiss anyone, check your headcount, how long they have worked for you, and whether you can show the Small Business Fair Dismissal Code steps on paper. Count regular casuals and staff in associated entities to confirm you are under 15 employees. In a small business, the minimum period before they can claim is 12 months. Following the Code is a complete defence, but it is not a free pass. You still need a valid reason, and for performance issues they must have had a prior warning. Write each step down as it happens, because records are what win at conciliation. The trap is thinking being small makes you safe. Claims are cheap to lodge, so even weak ones cost you time. If one arrives, decide whether to contest or settle, then fix the process that caused it.

### source_refs
Fair Work Commission - What is the Small Business Fair Dismissal Code?
Fair Work Act 2009 Part 3-2 (unfair dismissal)
Fair Work Act 2009 s388 (Small Business Fair Dismissal Code)

### escalation_guidance
Any dismissal, or a conciliation notice or claim once lodged, needs legal advice before you act or respond. Get advice especially if you are unsure about your headcount, the person's length of service, or whether your Code steps are documented, because a wrong move can lead to reinstatement or compensation orders.

## PM14
<!-- Managing psychosocial hazards (new Code) -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Treat psychosocial hazards like a trip hazard: identify them, assess them, control them and review them. Under WHS law this is a core duty, not a wellbeing extra, and small businesses are not exempt. Hazards include high workload, low job control, unclear roles, poor support, isolated work, aggressive customers, and bullying or harassment. Ask your staff what affects them, because consultation is part of the duty. Then fix the causes, for example roster changes, a clear reporting channel, training or a workload review. Write down what you found and did, and review it regularly and after any incident or complaint. The trap is handling problems as they come up, or treating stress as a personal issue for an EAP. The detail depends on your state or territory WHS law, and Victoria has separate but similar rules.

### source_refs
Safe Work Australia - Model Code of Practice: Managing psychosocial hazards at work
Safe Work Australia - Psychosocial hazards
Work Health and Safety (Managing Psychosocial Hazards at Work) Code of Practice 2024 (Cth), Federal Register of Legislation F2024L01380

### escalation_guidance
Any bullying, harassment or aggression complaint, any incident involving psychological injury, or any contact from a WHS regulator - get specialist WHS or legal advice before you act, because mishandling it can lead to improvement notices, investigation or prosecution.

## PM21
<!-- Burnout & mental-health absence/presenteeism -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
If you think someone is burnt out, have a supportive conversation before any performance one. Ask what is going on, what their workload looks like and what would help. The trap is reading disengagement, rising absence or slipping quality as an attitude problem and starting a performance improvement plan, when the cause may be the job itself. Burnout is not just a personal resilience issue. Excessive workload, poor support and low control are named psychosocial hazards, so this sits under your work health and safety duty. WHS rules come from your state or territory, not one national law. Having no EAP budget does not mean you have no options. Look at a workload review, flexible arrangements, low-cost EAP alternatives and free government or industry resources. Around half of Australian workers report burnout in the past year, so treat it as common, not rare.

### source_refs
Beyond Blue - 1 in 2 Australians Facing Workplace Burnout (media release, June 2025) https://www.beyondblue.org.au/about/media/media-releases/1-in-2-Australians-Facing-Workplace-Burnout
Corporate Mental Health Alliance Australia - Leading Mentally Healthy Workplaces Survey Report 2025 https://cmhaa.org.au/wp-content/uploads/CMHAA-Survey-Report-2025_digital_Final.pdf

### escalation_guidance
If burnout shows up as extended absence, a complaint, a WHS incident, or you are thinking about discipline or a performance plan, get advice before you act. A wrong step here mishandles a psychosocial hazard and an illness issue at the same time, and that creates WHS regulator exposure.
