## RS26
<!-- Weak handoff from recruitment to onboarding -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Before they start, run one short checklist that turns the candidate into an employee: signed contract, TFN declaration, super choice form, the Fair Work Information Statement, and their IT and access set up. Then carry everything you learnt in recruitment into their employee record. That means reference notes, the start terms you agreed, and any adjustment you promised. On day one, do a real induction that covers WHS and emergency procedures, not just a chat and a tour. Book the 30, 60 and 90-day check-ins and the probation review now. 'We'll sort the paperwork on day one' is the trap. Each late or missing form is a separate compliance gap. A promised adjustment that gets lost once they start can create fresh discrimination exposure. Early problems are cheapest to fix when you spot them in the first few weeks.

### source_refs
HQ.ai AU HR research report - J1. Weak handoff from recruitment to onboarding/People function

### escalation_guidance
Get advice before you act if you promised an adjustment during recruitment and now cannot or will not provide it, because withdrawing it can create discrimination exposure. Do the same if you find super or other paperwork was missed after the person has already been paid, because a missed obligation may need fixing properly rather than quietly.

## RS5
<!-- Inconsistent, unstructured phone screens -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Write a short script and use it for every candidate. Cover the same must-haves each time: availability, salary expectations, notice period, right to work, and the one or two non-negotiables for the role. Have the same person run the screens where you can, and jot down each answer straight after the call. A freeform 'quick chat to suss them out' feels efficient. But it makes candidates hard to compare fairly and lets rapport decide who goes through. The trap is availability. Asking about kids or caring responsibilities to work out whether someone can do the hours drifts into family status and discrimination risk. Questions about age and health carry the same risk. Ask the job question instead: 'The role needs Saturday shifts. Can you do that?' Keep the phone screen to basic fit, and save deeper questions for a structured interview.

### source_refs
HQ.ai AU HR research report - C1. Inconsistent, unstructured phone screens

### escalation_guidance
If a candidate complains that they were asked about family, age, health or similar, or that they were screened out for one of those reasons, get advice before you respond. Discrimination exposure applies at every hiring stage, including an informal phone call.

## RS2
<!-- AI-assisted resume screening & algorithmic bias -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Do not let the tool make the final call. Keep a person reviewing the shortlist, and have someone check the candidates the tool screened out, not just the ones it ranked highly. These tools learn from past hiring patterns and can filter people out on things that stand in for race, disability, age or sex, such as names, employment gaps, postcode or graduation year. The trap is trusting the vendor's "bias-free" claim. If the outcome is discriminatory, the law still holds you responsible, even if it was unintentional and the bias came from someone else's software. Federal discrimination laws and the Fair Work Act's adverse action rules all apply. Ask the vendor for testing and audit evidence. Tell candidates you use AI in screening. Work through the AHRC's AI and recruitment checklist before you rely on the tool.

### escalation_guidance
If a candidate complains they were screened out unfairly, or you suspect the tool is filtering by name, age, gaps or postcode, get advice before responding. A discriminatory outcome creates liability even when the bias came from the vendor's software.

## RS4
<!-- Undocumented, indefensible shortlisting -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Before you cut anyone, write down the criteria you are shortlisting against, and make them match the essential criteria in your ad. Then check every applicant against the same list and note one job-related reason for each person you drop. Skipping that record is the trap. Shortlisting is where 'why wasn't I even interviewed?' complaints start. Anti-discrimination law and the Fair Work Act's general protections apply at this stage, not just at dismissal. A rejected candidate can bring a claim over being screened out, and your only real defence is showing a consistent, criteria-based process. 'Not a culture fit' or 'I could just tell from the CV' is not a reason. Hold referred or known candidates to the same standard as strangers. If you can, get a second person to look at borderline cases before you decide.

### source_refs
Fair Work Act 2009 (Cth) s351
Fair Work Ombudsman - Workplace discrimination fact sheet
HQ.ai AU HR research report - B1. Undocumented, indefensible shortlisting decisions

### escalation_guidance
If a candidate complains, alleges discrimination or threatens a claim about not being shortlisted, get advice before you respond. General protections and discrimination claims apply to hiring decisions, and what you say next can become evidence.

## X9
<!-- Economics of outsourced HR/legal advice (what's displaced) -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: FALSE
- status: review

### answer_text
Before you sign up for paid HR support, count how many HR questions you had last year and buy for that, not for the worst case. There are three options: paying a lawyer by the hour for specific matters, paying an HR consultant by the hour or on a monthly retainer, and fixed-fee subscription services that bundle 'unlimited advice'. Hourly billing gets expensive if you ask lots of small questions. Retainers assume a monthly need that many small businesses do not have. Subscriptions can lock you into long contracts, and a major provider in this category has been penalised over misleading advertising, so read the term and exit clauses before you sign. The opposite trap is having no support and paying emergency rates every time, which over a year can cost more than a modest arrangement. Casual-heavy, high-turnover or multi-award workforces usually justify more structured help.

### source_refs
HQ.ai AU HR research report - 6.9 The Economics of Outsourced HR / Employment Legal Advice (What the Product Displaces)

### escalation_guidance
If the question in front of you involves a formal claim, a complaint, or a letter from a lawyer or union, pay for human advice now rather than weighing cost. Saving on fees is a false economy once legal exposure is live.
