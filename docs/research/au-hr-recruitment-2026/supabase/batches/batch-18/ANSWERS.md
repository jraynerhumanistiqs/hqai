## RA9
<!-- Credible EVP as a low-brand-recognition employer -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: FALSE
- status: review

### answer_text
Pick two or three real reasons someone would choose your business over a bigger one, and put them in the ad in plain words. Think flexibility, autonomy, room to grow, the team, the purpose of the work, or the security of a stable local business. Test each claim: could a current employee back it up? If not, leave it out. The trap is filler like "fun, fast-paced environment", which reads as padding. The other trap is promising what you do not deliver. People who join for flexibility that never appears tend to leave early, and in a small local labour market word travels, including through online company reviews. Decide what goes in the ad and what you save for the interview. Keep every claim true, because a false claim about the job itself could raise misleading-conduct concerns under the Australian Consumer Law.

### source_refs
Department of Employment and Workplace Relations - Employee Value Proposition (https://www.dewr.gov.au/employing-and-supporting-women-your-organisation/employee-value-proposition)

### escalation_guidance
If a candidate or new starter says the job was not what the ad promised, or you are unsure whether a claim about pay, conditions or the nature of the role is accurate, get advice before you run or repeat the ad. A materially false claim about the job could raise misleading-conduct concerns.

## RA10
<!-- "Ghost job" perception & trust erosion -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: FALSE
- status: review

### answer_text
Before you post, set a realistic time frame for replying to applicants and stick to it. Take the ad down as soon as the role is filled. To a candidate, a slow reply from a busy owner looks exactly like a ghost job, a listing with no real intent to hire. In a tight local market that reputation travels fast. There is no direct legal obligation in Australia to respond to every applicant, so the main risk is to your name rather than a claim. The exception is advertising a role you do not genuinely intend to fill. The Australian Consumer Law bans misleading conduct about employment, and that could apply. The trap is leaving ads live after you hire. Send a short acknowledgement and a short no-thanks. If volume is high, do not treat every applicant as equally serious.

### source_refs
Competition and Consumer Act 2010 (Cth) Schedule 2 (Australian Consumer Law) s31 - Misleading conduct relating to employment

### escalation_guidance
If you are thinking about advertising a role you do not intend to fill, for pipeline-building or to look like you are growing, get advice first because it may be misleading conduct under the Australian Consumer Law. Also get advice if a candidate or regulator alleges your ad was misleading.

## RA14
<!-- Gender pay equity exposure below WGEA threshold -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Do a simple check this month: list everyone doing comparable work, line up what they are paid, and make sure any difference has a reason you could explain out loud. Being under 100 staff means you do not have to report your gender pay gap to WGEA. It does not mean pay equity law skips you. The Fair Work Act principle of equal remuneration for work of equal or comparable value covers employers of every size, and sex discrimination law applies regardless of size too. The trap is setting each person's pay by one-off negotiation with no internal consistency check. That can quietly produce a gap between men and women. An affected employee, a union or the Sex Discrimination Commissioner can apply to the Fair Work Commission for an equal remuneration order. Simple pay bands for each role make pay much easier to keep fair and to explain.

### source_refs
Fair Work Ombudsman - Gender pay equity best practice guide
Workplace Gender Equality Agency - What is pay equity?
Workplace Gender Equality Agency - Publishing employer gender pay gaps
Fair Work Act 2009 Part 2-7 (Equal remuneration)
Sex Discrimination Act 1984 (Cth)

### escalation_guidance
If an employee, union or the Sex Discrimination Commissioner raises a pay gap, or your check finds a gap between men and women you cannot explain, get employment-law advice before changing anyone's pay. A rushed fix can look like an admission or create a discrimination claim.

## RA5
<!-- Role scope creep & level misalignment -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Write down what the role does, and what it does not do, before you hire. Then set a point where you check again if their duties grow. In a small team everyone wears a few hats, so some flexibility is normal. The trap is that extra work piles up quietly until they raise it, or it shows up as a resignation. When duties have materially expanded, decide whether the job now sits in a higher award classification. If it does and the pay has not moved, that can be an underpayment. Check the classification against the work they actually do, not their job title, using your award or the Fair Work Pay Calculator. Be careful too about loading on duties. Expanding a role unilaterally and unreasonably can feed an unfair dismissal or constructive dismissal claim.

### source_refs
INTHEBLACK (CPA Australia) - Job creep explained and expert advice for handling it
Fair Work Ombudsman - Pay Calculator

### escalation_guidance
Get advice before acting if their expanded duties may now fit a higher award classification without a pay change, because that is potential underpayment. Also get advice if they object to added duties or say the job has become a different role, because that carries constructive dismissal risk.

## RS24
<!-- Employee-records exemption uncertainty ("tranche two") -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: FALSE
- status: review

### answer_text
Treat candidate data as regulated from first contact, and write a separate process for how you collect, use, store and dispose of it. The employee-records exemption in the Privacy Act only covers acts and practices directly related to a current employee's employment relationship. It does not cover job applicants. The trap is thinking 'employee data is exempt anyway' and applying that to resumes, interview notes and screening results. Those fall under the Australian Privacy Principles, unless your business sits outside the Act under the small-business exemption, so check that with the OAIC. The exemption for current staff may also narrow. Reform known as 'tranche two' is considering narrowing or removing it, and the small-business exemption too, but this is proposed and under consultation, not law. Fix candidate handling now and keep watching the reform.

### source_refs
Privacy Act 1988 (Cth) s7B(3)
Office of the Australian Information Commissioner - Employment privacy rights

### escalation_guidance
If you are unsure whether the Privacy Act applies to your business, have had a candidate or staff data breach, or are using AI or third-party tools to screen applicants, get privacy advice before you rely on any exemption. Mishandling candidate data creates exposure the exemption will not protect you from.
