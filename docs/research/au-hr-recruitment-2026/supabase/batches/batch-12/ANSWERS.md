## PM18
<!-- Managing absenteeism without a system -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Keep a simple record of every absence: date, day of the week, reason given, and whether it was paid leave. Without a system, patterns like Mondays, Fridays or days after a public holiday are easy to feel and hard to prove. If a pattern shows up, have a private, supportive conversation first and ask how they are going, not why they keep letting you down. Look for what might sit behind it, such as workload, caring duties, health or stress at work. The trap is treating genuine sick or carer's leave as a conduct problem. That leave is a protected NES entitlement, and penalising it risks a general protections claim. Unauthorised absence is different and can be managed. If absence points to overwork or another hazard, your state or territory WHS duty applies. Only move to a formal process if the pattern continues after support is offered.

### source_refs
Fair Work Act 2009 Part 2-2 Division 7 (NES personal/carer's leave)
Fair Work Act 2009 ss340-361 (general protections)

### escalation_guidance
Before any warning, discipline or formal process over absence, or where the absences involve illness, injury, mental health, caring duties or a possible workplace hazard, get advice from an HR or employment-law professional. Treating protected leave as misconduct creates general protections exposure that cannot be undone.

## PM13
<!-- Right to Disconnect in daily practice -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Yes, this applies to you now, even if you have fewer than 15 staff. The small business exemption has ended. It is not a ban on after-hours contact. You can still text about tomorrow's shift. What changes is that staff can refuse to monitor, read or respond to work contact outside their hours, unless that refusal is unreasonable. Whether it is unreasonable depends on the facts: why you made contact, how you made it, how urgent it was, the person's role, whether they are paid to be available, and their personal circumstances. The trap is leaving 'reasonable' undefined. Sit down with your team and agree what after-hours contact looks like for each role, such as who gets rostering texts and what counts as urgent. Then write it down, as a policy or agreed norms. If a disagreement can't be sorted out, it can go to the Fair Work Commission.

### source_refs
Fair Work Ombudsman - New right to disconnect laws
Fair Work Ombudsman - Right to disconnect for small business employees starts 26 August

### escalation_guidance
If an employee has refused after-hours contact and you are thinking of taking any action against them because of it, or a dispute has gone to the Fair Work Commission, get advice before you act. Treating a refusal as misconduct without checking whether it was unreasonable can turn a rostering disagreement into a claim.

## PM2
<!-- Weak/absent structured onboarding -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Before they start, send the contract, the Fair Work Information Statement and, for casuals, the Casual Employment Information Statement, and set up their systems access. Then write down what good looks like in the role: a plan for the first week, a 30-day check-in with goals, a 60-day mid-point and a 90-day review timed to the end of probation. Keep a short note of each conversation. A buddy is fine, but it does not replace you checking in. The trap is that onboarding feels optional when you are busy, so it gets cut. If performance or conduct later becomes a problem, you will have nothing written to point to, and a warning, performance plan or dismissal becomes much harder to show was fair. Missing the information statements is a breach in its own right, and it weakens your position in any later dispute.

### source_refs
Fair Work Ombudsman - Before starting employment

### escalation_guidance
If a new starter's performance or conduct is already a concern and you are thinking about a warning, performance plan or ending their employment, especially near the end of probation, get advice before you act. Unfair dismissal and general protections claims are hard to defend without written expectations and a fair process.

## PM11
<!-- Notice periods & final pay errors -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Work out how long they have been continuously employed, then check the NES notice scale against their award and contract and use whichever is longer. NES notice rises with service up to four weeks, plus one extra week if they are over 45 with at least two years' service. Casuals get no notice. You can have them work the notice or pay it out instead. Then add any accrued annual leave they have not taken. The trap is thinking small business means no notice: small businesses are exempt from redundancy pay, not notice. When final pay must be paid can vary by state or territory, so check that too. Get it wrong and a clean termination becomes an underpayment claim, with back-pay and super on top. Check the amounts with your award or the Fair Work Pay Calculator.

### source_refs
Fair Work Ombudsman - Notice of termination and redundancy pay fact sheet
Fair Work Ombudsman - Dismissal and notice
Fair Work Act 2009 s117

### escalation_guidance
Get advice before you act if the termination is for serious misconduct without notice, if the dismissal itself could be challenged, or if you find a final pay was already underpaid. A notice or leave error adds an underpayment claim on top of any dismissal claim.

## PM15
<!-- Officer due diligence & right-sized WHS -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
If you are a director or owner who makes the big decisions, assume you are an 'officer' and that you personally carry a due diligence duty, separate from the business's own WHS duty. That means taking active steps, not trusting that someone has it covered. For a small business the basics are a simple safety policy, a way for staff to report hazards and incidents, emergency procedures, risk assessments for higher-risk tasks, and genuine consultation with workers. Consultation can be informal, but you have to actually seek their input and consider it. The trap is writing a policy once and never checking it is followed. Stay current on your WHS knowledge and regularly check the system is actually being used. The duty sits in your state or territory WHS Act, so details and penalties vary, and officers can be personally liable.

### source_refs
Safe Work Australia - What does an officer need to do?
Model Work Health and Safety Act s27 (officer due diligence) and state or territory equivalents

### escalation_guidance
Get legal advice as soon as there is a serious incident, injury, or contact from your WHS regulator. Officers can be personally liable, and in some states the most serious cases can lead to imprisonment.
