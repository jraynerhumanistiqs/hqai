## PF8
<!-- Which award (if any) applies -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Start with what your business actually does, then read the coverage clause (usually clause 4) of each award that looks close. Match each role to a classification by what the person mainly does, not their job title. If your business spans activities, like a café that also caters, different roles may sit under different awards. The trap is thinking that paying above minimum wage means the award doesn't matter. Penalty rates, allowances and other award clauses still apply unless a set-off is properly structured, and no one can contract out of an award. Genuinely award-free roles are rare, mostly high-income or managerial. If an enterprise agreement applies, it replaces the award for those staff. Re-check coverage whenever the business changes what it does. A wrong guess is underpayment, whatever your intent.

### source_refs
Fair Work Commission - Modern award coverage
Fair Work Ombudsman - About awards
Fair Work Act 2009 Part 2-3

### escalation_guidance
If your business spans more than one industry, a role doesn't clearly match any classification, or you are treating someone as award-free, get expert help before you set pay. A wrong coverage call creates underpayment exposure that applies regardless of intent and builds with every pay run.

## PF9
<!-- Getting the classification level right -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Pull out the award and read the classification descriptors against what the person actually does day to day, not their job title or what you hired them for. Classification is judged by the principal purpose of the role, so a 'junior' who now runs the store may sit at a higher level. Check this at hiring and again whenever duties change, because roles drift and pay often does not follow. That drift is the trap. Each pay period at the wrong level adds to a back-pay bill, and underpayment is strict liability, so not knowing is no defence. If duties have moved up, reclassify and pay the correct minimum from now, then work out what is owed for the past. Do not quietly fix it going forward and hope. Check the current rate for the level in the Fair Work Pay Calculator or your award.

### source_refs
Fair Work Act 2009 s45
JB Solicitors - Fair Work Awards: Your Complete Guide (https://jbsolicitors.com.au/employment-awards/)
Australian Industry Group - Understanding modern award coverage (https://www.australianindustrygroup.com.au/resourcecentre/resource-centre-blogs/hr-blogs/understanding-modern-award-coverage/)

### escalation_guidance
If someone has been working above their classification for a while, or you are unsure which level fits, get advice before you reclassify or calculate back pay. Getting the level or the back-pay amount wrong is an underpayment exposure with penalties.

## PF3
<!-- Employee vs contractor - new "whole of relationship" test -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Pull out every contractor arrangement you have and compare the paperwork with how the work actually happens. Under the Fair Work Act, what counts now is the real substance and practical reality of the whole relationship, not just the contract wording. An ABN, invoices and a contractor agreement do not settle it. The trap is drift. Someone engaged as a contractor years ago may now work set hours, use your tools and work only for you. The test applies for as long as the relationship runs, so an arrangement that was fine at the start may not be fine now. The older contract-first test still applies under some other laws, so the answer can differ depending on which law is asking. Getting it wrong can mean unpaid NES entitlements, super guarantee charge, PAYG withholding and possible sham contracting penalties.

### source_refs
Fair Work Ombudsman - Closing Loopholes
Fair Work Act 2009 s15AA

### escalation_guidance
If anyone you treat as a contractor works set hours, uses your equipment or works only for you, or if you are thinking of reclassifying them, get advice before you act. Misclassification exposure covers unpaid entitlements, super and tax, and can extend to sham contracting penalties.

## PF6
<!-- Casual employment - new statutory definition -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Look at how each casual actually works, not what their letter says. A person is only casual if there is no firm advance commitment to continuing and indefinite work, judged on the practical reality of the relationship. The trap is the long-term casual on the same regular, predictable roster, the classic retail and hospitality pattern. That pattern now risks them being treated as permanent, with back-pay of leave entitlements on top of the casual loading you already paid. Length of service alone does not decide it; the commitment does. Update casual contracts and letters to reflect the new definition, check your award's casual conversion clause, and decide whether to offer permanent work or wait for the employee to use the Employee Choice Pathway. Set-off rules for loading already paid may reduce the exposure, but do not rely on them.

### source_refs
Fair Work Ombudsman - Closing Loopholes
Fair Work Act 2009 Part 2-2 Div 4A
Aitken Partners - Casual employment under the Closing the Loopholes legislation

### escalation_guidance
If a long-term casual on a regular roster disputes their status, asks about conversion, or raises leave back-pay, get advice before you respond. A wrong call here can mean back-paying leave entitlements and an underpayment dispute.

## PF1
<!-- Do I need a written employment contract? -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Put it in writing, even though the law does not always demand it. For most ongoing staff the Fair Work Act does not require a written contract, but one is effectively needed to properly engage casuals, fixed-term staff and anyone on an individual flexibility arrangement. Whatever you agree, give every new employee the Fair Work Information Statement when they start, as missing it can attract a civil penalty. Spell out their hours, classification and the award that covers them, because a contract silent on these causes disputes later. The trap is the old template from Google. The NES and the modern award sit underneath every contract, and any term below them is void and replaced by the minimum. The void term does not save you: it leaves you exposed to back-pay and penalties. Check the award or the Fair Work Pay Calculator before anyone signs.

### source_refs
Fair Work Ombudsman - About employment contracts
Fair Work Ombudsman - Employment contracts
Fair Work Act 2009 s125

### escalation_guidance
If an existing contract or template may pay below the award or NES, or you are unsure which award or classification applies, get advice before acting. A sub-minimum term creates back-pay and penalty exposure.
