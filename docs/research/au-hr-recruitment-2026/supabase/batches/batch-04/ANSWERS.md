## PM20
<!-- Positive duty (Sex Discrimination Act) at SME scale -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Treat this as applying to you. The positive duty under the Sex Discrimination Act covers every employer, with no small-business exemption. You must take reasonable and proportionate steps to prevent sexual harassment, sex-based harassment, sex discrimination and related victimisation before anything happens, not just respond after a complaint. For a small team, proportionate steps include a short written policy, a clear way for staff to raise concerns, covering expectations at onboarding, a periodic refresher, and a prompt process for handling any complaint. The standard flexes with your size; the duty does not. The trap is a policy nobody has seen. Keep records showing it was communicated, trained on and enforced, because that is your evidence of compliance. 'We've never had a complaint' does not show compliance. The Australian Human Rights Commission can inquire without a complaint and has flagged retail and hospitality as priority industries.

### source_refs
Sex Discrimination Act 1984 (Cth) s47C
Australian Human Rights Commission - What are your obligations? (humanrights.gov.au/know-your-rights/obligations-as-a-business-and-organisation/what-are-your-obligations)
Fair Work Ombudsman - Rules about workplace sexual harassment

### escalation_guidance
Any complaint or report of harassment or discrimination, or any contact from the Australian Human Rights Commission, needs professional advice before you respond. A mishandled response can create victimisation and enforcement exposure on top of the original issue.

## PM6
<!-- Progressive discipline & procedural fairness -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Before you warn anyone, write down the specific allegation, check the facts, and give them written notice of a meeting with time to prepare (commonly 24 to 48 hours) and the option to bring a support person. Do not refuse one. In the meeting, genuinely listen to their side before you decide anything. Then record the outcome and when any warning will be reviewed. Standard practice is graduated: verbal warning, written warning, final written warning, then termination, scaled to how serious the issue is. Jumping to a final warning for a first minor issue is a common mistake. So is doing nothing until it blows up. Treat every staff member the same way, because inconsistency can look like discrimination. The trap: a poor process can make a dismissal unfair even when the conduct really happened. Under 15 staff? Follow the Small Business Fair Dismissal Code checklist.

### source_refs
Fair Work Commission - Dismissal rules for small business owners
Fair Work Act 2009 s387
Small Business Fair Dismissal Code

### escalation_guidance
Any discipline where dismissal is a possible outcome, where you are the person who was wronged, or where it lands close in time to a complaint, leave, illness, injury or pregnancy - get advice before you act. A rushed or inconsistent process can turn real misconduct into an unfair dismissal or adverse action claim.

## PM12
<!-- Flexible working arrangement requests -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Before you say no, talk it through with them. The Fair Work Act requires you to discuss the request and genuinely try to reach agreement on an alternative before you refuse. Reply in writing within 21 days. Eligible employees include parents, carers, people over 55, people with a disability and people experiencing family and domestic violence. You can only refuse on reasonable business grounds specific to the role and your business, such as cost, capacity, practicality, efficiency or customer-service impact. 'We're too small' is the trap: business size is one factor to weigh, not an automatic reason to refuse. If you refuse, write down the grounds and the alternatives you considered. A verbal no with no paper trail can now go to the Fair Work Commission, which can order you to grant the request.

### source_refs
Fair Work Ombudsman - Requests for flexible working arrangements fact sheet
Fair Work Act 2009 ss65-65A

### escalation_guidance
If the request comes from someone who is pregnant, returning from parental leave, has a disability or illness, or is experiencing family and domestic violence, or if they have lodged a dispute with the Fair Work Commission, get advice before refusing. A poorly handled refusal can be overturned by the Commission and may be linked to other claims.

## PM1
<!-- Probation / minimum-employment-period confusion -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Treat probation as a check-in point in the contract, not a free pass to dismiss. Probation has no standing under the Fair Work Act. The legal clock that matters is the minimum employment period: 6 months, or 12 months if you are a small business with fewer than 15 employees. Until that period passes, the person generally cannot make an unfair dismissal claim, and that is all it changes. They still get full leave and other NES entitlements from day one. General protections and discrimination claims have no waiting period at all. Timing is the trap. Letting someone go soon after they raise a complaint, a safety issue or a leave request can look like adverse action, probation or not. So always have a documented reason and a fair conversation. If you are unsure about someone, extend probation in writing before it lapses.

### source_refs
Fair Work Ombudsman - Probation
Fair Work Ombudsman - Before starting employment
Fair Work Act 2009 Part 3-1 (general protections)

### escalation_guidance
Any dismissal during probation or early tenure, especially soon after a complaint, safety issue, leave request, illness or pregnancy, needs advice before you act. General protections claims apply from day one with uncapped compensation.

## PM4
<!-- No feedback / no documentation trail -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Start writing things down now. Each time you raise an issue, make a short dated file note of what you said, the specific standard they are missing, and their response. Mentioning it a few times with no note is the trap - it is hard to show a warning happened if nothing was written. Vague feedback like 'pick up your game' cannot be tied to a measurable standard later, so name the actual gap. Act at the first pattern, not when you are furious. Documenting is not a paper trail against you; having no trail is the risk. Under the unfair dismissal test in the Fair Work Act and the Small Business Fair Dismissal Code, the employer must show the person was warned and given a chance to improve before a performance-based dismissal. Without that, a dismissal can be found unfair even when the concern was genuine.

### source_refs
Fair Work Commission - Warnings - unsatisfactory performance
Fair Work Act 2009 s387
Small Business Fair Dismissal Code

### escalation_guidance
If you are thinking about a formal warning, a PIP or dismissal, or are unsure whether the issue is conduct or capacity, get advice before you act. Moving to dismissal with no documented warning and no chance to improve is the most common way these dismissals are found unfair.
