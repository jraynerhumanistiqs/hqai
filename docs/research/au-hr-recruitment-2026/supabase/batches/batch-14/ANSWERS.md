## RA1
<!-- Whether to hire at all (backfill vs redesign vs automate) -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Before you advertise, pause long enough to check the need is real. Work through it in order: confirm the work will keep coming, list the essential tasks that must be covered, decide whether you need temporary cover or a permanent hire, then decide whether the role should keep its old shape or be redesigned. Last, check your cash flow can carry an ongoing wage. The trap is auto-backfilling. The person who left may have been in a role that was never right-sized, and replacing them can buy time without fixing a broken process. Ask whether this is a hiring problem or a systems problem. Also check the hours: the real gap is sometimes 15 to 20 hours a week, not a full-time job. A rushed, poorly scoped role can later cause underpayment, sham contracting or discrimination problems.

### source_refs
HQ.ai AU HR research report - A1. Deciding whether to hire at all (backfill vs redesign vs absorb vs automate)

### escalation_guidance
Get advice before you act if your plan changes an existing employee's duties or hours, removes or merges a role, or covers the work with a contractor instead of an employee. A wrong move there can turn into a redundancy, underpayment or sham contracting claim.

## RS23
<!-- Privacy Act ADM transparency rule (from 10 Dec 2026) -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: FALSE
- status: review

### answer_text
List every point in your hiring process where software screens out candidates, ranks them, or decides who gets an interview. A new Privacy Act rule requires your privacy policy to say what kinds of personal information feed those automated decisions and what kinds of decisions they are. The privacy regulator is reading 'automated decision' broadly, and deciding who gets an interview is likely in scope. The trap is thinking the vendor carries this. It does not sit with them alone, so ask the vendor in writing what candidate data the tool uses and what it decides. While you are at it, stop collecting data 'in case it's useful', store what you keep securely, and set a deletion schedule for rejected candidates, including video and audio recordings. Update the policy before the rule starts, and confirm the start date on the OAIC website.

### source_refs
Office of the Australian Information Commissioner - Guidance on privacy and the use of commercially available AI products
Privacy Act 1988 (Cth) Sch 1 - Australian Privacy Principles, APP 1 (new APP 1.7-1.9 automated decision-making transparency)
HQ.ai AU HR research report - I2. Privacy Act obligations for AI recruitment tools and the new ADM transparency rule

### escalation_guidance
If a candidate complains about how an automated tool assessed them, if candidate data is lost or breached, or if you cannot get clear answers from your vendor about what the tool uses and decides, get privacy advice before you respond. Screening tools that filter out candidates can also raise discrimination risk, which needs a human to look at it.

## RS19
<!-- Two-way candidate ghosting -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: FALSE
- status: review

### answer_text
Set up a basic automated status update so every applicant hears something, even if it is only 'we received your application' and 'this role has been filled'. Then shorten the gap between final interview and signed offer, and check in with your new starter a few days before day one to confirm they are still coming. Ghosting now runs both ways. Candidates apply with one click, treat any single application lightly, and will take a faster offer without telling you. Employers with big applicant pools often reply to almost nobody. The trap is thinking silence costs you nothing. In a small local labour market it hurts your reputation and can turn up in online reviews. Keep a simple record of who applied and what you decided, so you can show your process was fair if anyone questions it.

### source_refs
HQ.ai AU HR research report - H1. Two-way candidate ghosting

### escalation_guidance
If an unsuccessful applicant formally complains that your selection process was unfair, or asks why they were rejected, get advice before you respond. A pattern of never replying can make it harder to show you ran a fair process.

## RS20
<!-- Slow time-to-hire losing candidates -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: FALSE
- status: review

### answer_text
Before you advertise, decide who can make the final call, how many interview rounds you genuinely need, and the longest gap you will allow between stages. Then stick to it. Most SMEs lose good candidates to a process that drags, not to a bad offer. Usually only the owner can sign off, interviews get bumped by the day job, and nobody is chasing it along. Run approvals side by side rather than one after another. If you cannot get everyone in a room, give one person authority to decide. When you make a verbal offer, have the written offer ready to follow quickly. The trap is treating a strong candidate as safe. They are the one most likely to take a faster offer, so move with more urgency, not less.

### source_refs
HQ.ai AU HR research report - H2. Slow time-to-hire losing candidates to faster movers

### escalation_guidance
This is mainly a commercial efficiency issue. If the pressure to move fast tempts you to make an offer before the pay, classification or contract terms are settled, stop and check those first, because a rushed offer can lock in the wrong terms.

## RS21
<!-- Cost-per-hire pressure & agency-fee dependency -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: FALSE
- status: review

### answer_text
Before you call an agency, work out the full cost of the hire, not just the fee. Add the placement fee, your own time briefing and interviewing, and the cost of onboarding the person once they start. Then decide role by role. Simpler roles you can often fill yourself through job boards, while senior or hard-to-fill roles may justify paying someone else to search. If you do use an agency, negotiate the replacement guarantee before you sign. That is the trap. Many owners only read that clause after a placement falls over, then pay again to repeat the process. Ask how long the guarantee runs and exactly what it covers. Use a specialist agency for a specialised role, not a generalist. This is commercial risk rather than legal risk, but read the agency contract properly before you commit.

### source_refs
HQ.ai AU HR research report - H3. Cost-per-hire pressure and agency-fee dependency

### escalation_guidance
If the agency terms of business include large upfront or non-refundable fees, long exclusivity, or fees triggered if you later hire a candidate directly, have the contract reviewed before signing. A poorly understood agency contract can lock you into paying twice for one role.
