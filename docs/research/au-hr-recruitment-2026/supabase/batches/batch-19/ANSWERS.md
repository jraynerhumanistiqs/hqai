## RS25
<!-- No AI governance / vendor oversight in SME recruitment -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Write a one-page rule for how AI is used in your hiring before you next advertise. Say which steps the tool may assist with and which decisions only a person makes, such as rejecting or shortlisting. Keep a simple record of where the tool influenced an outcome. Every few months, sample those outcomes for any group of candidates being screened out more than you would expect. Before switching on an AI feature, ask the vendor for validation evidence, how they test for bias, and how they handle candidate data. The trap is assuming the platform sorts that out. You carry the responsibility, not the vendor. Having no policy is not itself a breach. But with no record of reasonable care, a bias or privacy complaint becomes hard to defend. Decide now who handles a candidate who challenges a decision.

### source_refs
HQ.ai AU HR research report - I4. Lack of AI governance and vendor oversight in SME recruitment
Australian HR Institute (AHRI) - Australia needs better AI regulation, says Human Rights Commission

### escalation_guidance
If a candidate complains that an AI-influenced decision was biased or that their data was mishandled, get advice before you respond. At that point it is a discrimination or privacy exposure, not a process tidy-up.

## RS10
<!-- Disproportionate/irrelevant psychometric testing -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Before you test anyone, write down what the role actually needs and only use a test that measures that. If you cannot say what the test measures and why it predicts performance in this job, drop it. A generic personality test run on every role, often because the software bundled it in, adds cost and slows hiring without telling you much. The trap is treating a score as a pass or fail gate. Use results as one input alongside your own judgement, and apply the same test and threshold to every candidate for that role. Ask candidates whether they need an adjustment, because the Disability Discrimination Act obligation to make reasonable adjustments applies to assessments. An unvalidated test used as a hard cut-off is hard to defend if someone claims it unfairly screened out a group. State discrimination laws may apply too.

### source_refs
Disability Discrimination Act 1992 (Cth)
HQ.ai AU HR research report - E1. Disproportionate or irrelevant psychometric/skills testing

### escalation_guidance
If a candidate with disability asks for an adjustment to a test, or anyone complains that a test screened them out unfairly, get advice before you respond. A mishandled adjustment request or a hard cut-off you cannot justify creates discrimination exposure.

## RS11
<!-- Reasonable adjustments in assessments (disability) -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Ask every candidate, at every stage, whether they need any adjustment to take part in a test or interview. Under the Disability Discrimination Act 1992, adjusting for a candidate with disability is a legal requirement, not a courtesy. That might mean extra time, a different format, or a different assessment that still measures the same job skill. The trap is 'we run the same test for everyone': failing to adjust a process that disadvantages someone because of disability can itself be unlawful discrimination. Do not read silence as no need, because many people will not raise it unless invited. There is a limit where an adjustment would be too heavy a burden on the business, but read it narrowly, not as a way out. The same duty applies to AI-driven assessments, and state discrimination laws can also apply.

### source_refs
Disability Discrimination Act 1992 (Cth)
JobAccess - Guidelines on reasonable adjustment
JobAccess - Interviewing people with disability
AFDO - What are Reasonable Adjustments?
HQ.ai AU HR research report - E2. Reasonable adjustments in assessments for candidates with disability

### escalation_guidance
If a candidate asks for an adjustment you think you cannot give, or you are about to reject someone after they disclosed a disability, get advice before you decide. A refusal or rejection here can be unlawful disability discrimination.

## RS15
<!-- Qualification/credential verification gaps -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: FALSE
- status: review

### answer_text
Decide now which roles get their qualifications checked directly with the issuing body, then do it every time. Licensed trades and regulated professions, such as health roles, should always be verified, not just sighted. For other roles, verify whenever the qualification is a stated requirement of the job. The trap is taking a copy or PDF at face value. Sighting the original is not verification. Organised fraud operations sell certificates claiming to be from real TAFEs and universities, and these can look convincing at a glance. Contact the institution or use a verification service. Also decide whether you check before or after making an offer, and apply that consistently. For general roles, a fake credential is mainly a fraud and negligent-hire risk. For licensed work it can also carry safety, licensing and even criminal exposure.

### source_refs
HQ.ai AU HR research report - F4. Qualification and credential verification gaps

### escalation_guidance
If you find a fake or unverifiable qualification held by someone already working for you, get advice before you act, because any move to end their employment carries dismissal risk. If they hold a fake qualification for licensed trade or regulated work, also get advice straight away, because the safety, licensing and criminal exposure goes beyond ordinary HR.

## RS6
<!-- One-way AI video interviews - backlash & legal footing -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Before you use one-way video interviews, decide whether a person or the platform's AI makes the call. The safer setup is a human reviewing every recording, not screening people out on an AI score alone. Tell candidates up front if AI is involved, what happens to the video, how long it is kept and when it is deleted. Ask the vendor whether footage is reused to train their models. Offer another format, such as a phone or live video chat, to anyone who asks because of disability or access needs. The trap is assuming the platform carries the risk. The Privacy Act and the Australian Privacy Principles apply to the personal information you collect this way, and face and voice recordings call for extra care. New automated decision-making transparency rules will also require your privacy policy to cover decisions like an AI screen-out.

### source_refs
Privacy Act 1988 (Cth) Schedule 1 - Australian Privacy Principles
HQ.ai AU HR research report - C2. One-way AI video interviews - candidate backlash and unclear legal footing

### escalation_guidance
If a candidate asks for an alternative format because of disability, complains about how their recording or data was used, or a screen-out rests on the AI score alone, get advice before you respond. Refusing an accessible alternative or mishandling face and voice data creates discrimination and privacy exposure.
