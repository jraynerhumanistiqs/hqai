## RS12
<!-- Reference checks - legal risk & low value -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: FALSE
- status: review

### answer_text
Write one short reference script and use it for every candidate for the role. Ask the same job-relevant questions the same way, and note what was actually said. Decide how many referees you need, and require that at least one is a direct former manager, so you are not only hearing from people the candidate hand-picked. Expect little. Many referees will only confirm dates and job title, because both sides worry about defamation if they say something negative they cannot back up. The trap is filling that silence with a 'vibe' and letting it decide the hire. Base your decision on documented, factual content, and record how the references fed into it. A fair, consistent process is what makes your hiring decision defensible. When you are the referee, stick to facts you can substantiate.

### source_refs
HQ.ai AU HR research report - F1. Reference checks: legal risk and low-value information

### escalation_guidance
If a referee makes serious allegations about a candidate, or you are asked to give a negative reference about a former employee, get advice before you rely on it or say it. Unsubstantiated negative statements create defamation exposure for both employers and referees.

## RS13
<!-- Police/criminal checks as an overreaching filter -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Before you order a police check, decide whether this role actually needs one, and write down why. Do not run checks on everyone just because a screening package bundles them in by default. Get the candidate's consent before you run any check. If a record comes back, look at that specific offence against the inherent requirements of this specific role, and record your reasoning. 'Any record means no job' is the trap. It feels safer, but automatically excluding someone without looking at relevance can be unlawful discrimination in some states and territories, where criminal record is a protected attribute. It can also support a general protections claim. Whether criminal record is protected depends on where you operate, so check your state or territory anti-discrimination law before relying on a blanket policy.

### source_refs
HQ.ai AU HR research report - F2. Police/criminal history checks used as a blunt, overreaching filter

### escalation_guidance
Before you withdraw an offer or reject a candidate because of something on their police check, get advice. Discrimination and general protections exposure depends on your state or territory and on how relevant the offence is to the role.

## RS9
<!-- Interview scorecard design & panel calibration -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Have every panellist score the candidate on their own, in writing, before anyone talks. Then compare scores and discuss the gaps. Keep the scorecard short: four to six competencies tied to the actual job, each rated on an anchored four-point scale where every point describes what that level looks like. A four-point scale removes the middle option people hide in. Add an evidence field under each score and require a specific example of what the candidate said or did. Drop vague items like 'culture fit' as a standalone criterion. The trap is talking it through first. In a small panel, the owner or most senior person speaks and everyone else drifts towards their view. A well-designed scorecard used the same way for every candidate is a practical defence if a hiring decision is later challenged as discriminatory. A sloppy one only looks rigorous.

### source_refs
HQ.ai AU HR research report - D3. Interview scorecard design and panel calibration

### escalation_guidance
If an unsuccessful candidate complains or alleges discrimination in the hiring decision, get advice before you respond. Keep the original individual scorecards and evidence notes, and do not rewrite them.

## RS17
<!-- Probation period misunderstanding -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: TRUE
- status: review

### answer_text
Treat probation as a check-in point in the contract, not a legal shield. Probation is a contractual idea that the Fair Work Act does not define or require. For unfair dismissal, what counts is the minimum employment period: generally six months, or 12 months if you are a small business with fewer than 15 employees. Your probation length and that period often do not line up. The trap is thinking "they're still on probation, so we can just let them go." General protections apply from day one. If you end someone's employment for a prohibited reason, they can bring a claim no matter how new they are or whether probation has finished. During probation, give honest feedback, keep notes, and base any decision on performance or conduct. When they pass, confirm it in writing so nobody is unsure where they stand.

### source_refs
HQ.ai AU HR research report - G2. Probation period misunderstanding
Fair Work Act 2009 ss340-361
Fair Work Act 2009 s383

### escalation_guidance
If you are thinking of ending employment during probation, get advice before you act. This matters most if the person has recently raised a concern, taken leave, been ill or injured, or told you they are pregnant, because general protections claims apply regardless of length of service.

## RS18
<!-- Counter-offers & lost candidates at the eleventh hour -->

- jurisdiction: federal
- is_escalate_default: FALSE
- status: review

### answer_text
Once you have picked someone, get a written offer out as soon as you can after the final interview, and sort approvals before you interview, not after. Speed is the trap. Every day the offer waits for sign-off gives their current employer time to counter. Build some negotiation room into the offer upfront, and ask early what else matters to them, because flexibility and development often count for more than salary. If they still pull out, let them go gracefully and keep your runner-up warm. The same logic applies when a valued person resigns from you. A counter-offer rarely fixes why they wanted to leave, and research suggests most people who accept one still move on within about two years. Fix the root cause instead. Keep offers and acceptances in writing so nobody is unsure whether a deal was done.

### source_refs
HQ.ai AU HR research report - G3. Counter-offers and lost candidates at the eleventh hour

### escalation_guidance
Get advice before you act if there is a dispute about whether an offer was accepted, or if you want to withdraw an offer someone has already accepted. Drawn-out negotiations can blur whether a binding agreement exists.
